Feature Story

The Windows 10 Deadline Is Two Months Away — Here's Why It Matters

Microsoft ended standard support for Windows 10 in October 2025, and the safety net is about to get expensive. Extended Security Updates (ESU) — the paid program that keeps Windows 10 machines receiving critical security patches — enters its steepest phase on October 14, 2026. For businesses, ESU pricing is designed to punish delay: roughly $61 per device in year one, doubling to $122 in year two, and doubling again to $244 in year three. Any PC left on Windows 10 without ESU stops receiving patches entirely, meaning every newly discovered vulnerability stays open forever. That's not just a security problem — it's a compliance one. Frameworks like HIPAA, PCI DSS, and most cyber-insurance policies require supported, patched software, and unsupported machines can quietly void your coverage. The good news: most hardware from the last few years upgrades to Windows 11 cleanly, and the ones that don't are usually cheaper to replace than to cover with escalating ESU fees. With two months to go, now is the time to inventory every device, confirm what upgrades and what gets replaced, and build a plan before the cliff arrives. If you're not sure where your fleet stands, that's exactly the conversation to have with us this month.

Cybersecurity

The "Deepfake CEO" Scam: Voice Cloning Is the New Wire Fraud

Business email compromise has a frightening new twist. Attackers can now clone an executive's voice from as little as three seconds of public audio — a podcast clip, a conference talk, a voicemail greeting — and use it to call finance staff and authorize urgent wire transfers. In one widely reported case, a finance worker sent roughly $25 million after joining a video call where every "colleague," including the CFO, was a real-time deepfake. Caller ID and "I'll just call them back" no longer prove anything, because those channels are exactly what attackers spoof. The defense is a rule, not a gadget: any payment change or unusual transfer request must be confirmed through a second, pre-agreed channel — a known internal code word or a callback to a number already on file, never the number in the request. Make it policy, and make it okay for staff to slow down and verify.

Identity & Access

Passwords Are on the Way Out — Passkeys Are Taking Over

The shift away from passwords is no longer theoretical. Starting September 1, 2026, Microsoft is making passkeys the default sign-in method in Microsoft 365 / Entra ID and phasing out easily-phished SMS and voice codes. Passkeys use the fingerprint, face, or PIN already on your device, and because there's no password to steal or reuse, they shut down the phishing and credential-stuffing attacks behind most breaches. Organizations that have rolled them out report dramatically faster logins and far fewer password-reset tickets. You don't have to flip everything overnight — the smart path is a hybrid rollout, enabling passkeys alongside existing logins and starting with your highest-risk accounts (email, banking, admin). If you'd like a plan for introducing passkeys to your team this fall, we can map it out.

Tip of the Month — Vacation Season

Don't Let Summer Coverage Gaps Become Security Gaps

August is peak vacation season, and attackers know it. When a manager or owner is out, the usual "let me double-check with them" safeguard disappears — which is exactly when fraudulent invoice and wire-transfer requests tend to land. A few quick habits close the gap: keep out-of-office replies vague (no travel dates, no "reach my assistant for approvals" details that hand attackers a script), designate a clear backup approver for payments before anyone leaves, and remind staff that an urgent request arriving while the boss is unreachable is a reason to slow down, not speed up. A five-minute heads-up before someone heads out the door can prevent a very expensive mistake.
